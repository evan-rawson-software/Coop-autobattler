Screens of a run

Layout sketches for the screens of a run, drawn for a 1080p screen. Screens 1 to 4, 7 and 8 repeat every round; 5, 6 and 9 start and end a run. Specific numbers and example effects in the sketches are placeholders, not rules. Your base is at the bottom and the enemy is at the top, so the front edge runs across the screen and a bigger party makes the board wider, not taller. Sizes, numbers, and building names are placeholders.

1. Battle

Nobody gives orders here. The party watches its factory start cold, ramp up, and send units across the neutral zone while the enemy does the same. Each player has their own Core, so each side shows one health bar per Core under a total. The sketch is a three-player party; a solo battle shows one bar per side.

  1. Who you are fighting and what kind of battle it is. A ghost shows the other party's names; a boss says so and names the boss. Boss-only marks are in sketch 1b.
  2. Your board, at the bottom, exactly as you built it. Conduits light up as resources flow.
  3. The neutral zone. Its lakes and cliffs are generated for this battle and revealed when it starts. Buildings do nothing here; only units fight.
  4. The enemy board, at the top, with one Core per player. Your units attack buildings in their way and finally the Cores: first the one opposite their owner’s plot, then the nearest still standing.
  5. Units from both sides meet and form a front line.
  6. A Core that has fallen: greyed out with a cross in its side’s colour. Its player’s buildings stay on the board.
  7. The total Core health and how many Cores stand, then one bar per player’s Core, and how many walls still stand. A fallen Core reads “(fallen)”. The enemy’s are in the matching panel on the right. A side loses when all of its Cores are destroyed.
  8. Live production and army counts, so you can see the ramp.
  9. Army strength over time for both sides, and, against a PvE stronghold, the Overload line: which side’s last Core Overload would destroy first, and when.
  10. Speed controls. In co-op, a change applies once everyone agrees.
  11. A log of the moments that mattered, such as a junction being destroyed or a Core falling (“Your Core 2 fell”).
  12. Camera presets (Center, My base, Their base, Fit battle). It starts at the center of the map, and the view pans and zooms, so a larger map or a four-player battle still works on the same screen.
  13. A Golem leads the push. It is drawn bigger than other units: huge health and flat armor, but slow and weak in attack.
  14. A caster’s blast hits a group: units near the target take part of the hit. Units only, no buildings, no friendly fire.

1b. Battle: boss marks

A boss battle looks like screen 1, with one extra mark for the boss in play. Each boss changes only its own side, never the party's factory. Four marks are sketched together here; a real battle shows only the one for its boss. All names are placeholders.

  1. Brood stronghold: each boss Core bar has ticks at 75%, 50% and 25%. When a Core falls below one, it releases a burst of units, and the log says so.
  2. Linked Cores: one shared bar for all the boss Cores, with the Cores listed under it. Damage to any Core comes off the bar, and they all fall together.
  3. Pylons: the shield pylons drawn around a boss Core, a shield ring on the Core while any stands, and a count (“Pylons 2/4”). Your units go for the pylons first.
  4. Warlord: the champion leading each wave, drawn bigger than a Golem and marked as the champion, with its own health bar.

1c. Battle: points of interest

The neutral zone of a four-player battle, drawn flat, with one point in each plot band (CB-8c to CB-8g). A real battle shows the zone between the two boards as on screen 1. Points sit on the middle row. The sketch shows four moments at once, and a real battle shows whatever is happening. Numbers are placeholders.

  1. A guarded point: neutral guards hold it and fight units of both sides that come near. Nothing can be captured while a guard lives.
  2. A point being captured: your units keep to its capture range, and the ring in your colour fills as they hold it. Both sides in range means no progress.
  3. A held point: a solid marker in the holder's colour with its buff icon. A buff applies to every player of the side.
  4. The healing area: the holder's units inside it slowly regain health.
  5. A point held by the enemy, with the strength icon: their new units are stronger while they hold it. Your units that pass near it divert to retake it.
  6. The buffs your side has now, and what each point is doing. A band may have no point.

2. Event choice

After every battle the party picks one of up to three events. It opens with the battle result so the reward is seen before the next decision.

  1. Result of the battle just fought and what it paid.
  2. Up to three offered events (never two of the same type). Each says exactly what it does before you choose.
  3. Votes from each player. The highlighted card is your own vote; you can change it until the last player has voted.
  4. Who has voted and how a split vote is settled (one vote is picked at random). There is no time limit; the event resolves once everyone has voted.
  5. A drawback has its own marked line, shown before the vote. It is always opt-in: take both or skip, and skipping never harms the party (a Pact).
  6. An offer names what it needs up front, such as the resource of a Rich vein, and may show the first round it appears from.

2b. Caravan draft

A Caravan is not a vote. It lists one more reward than there are players. Players choose one each, in turn; each reward is taken once and the last one is left. The order rotates from one Caravan to the next. At most one reward is party-wide. Rewards are placeholders.

  1. The reward list: players + 1 rewards. A taken reward shows who took it. The last one is left over.
  2. The party-wide reward (at most one) is marked and says it applies to everyone.
  3. Whose turn it is. You choose only when it is your turn.
  4. The order for this Caravan. It rotates from one Caravan to the next.
  5. Rewards go to the player who took them. Nothing comes from a teammate.

2c. Rich vein

Each player may add one node of the named resource on an empty cell of their own plot, or skip. The type is random and is stated on the offer before the vote.

  1. Your own plot. The green cells are the legal ones: empty, buildable cells.
  2. The resource type for this vein, named before the vote.
  3. Nodes added so far this run: at most 3 per plot.
  4. Skip. Skipping costs nothing.

2d. Blueprint

Each player chooses one of three rare buildings that are eligible at this round. It is not free: it waits in the next shop.

  1. Three rare buildings. Each card shows what it needs and what it does, as in the shop.
  2. Your pick. Each player picks for themselves.
  3. The note: the building is held in a fixed slot of your next shop that re-rolls do not replace, and it is still paid for.
  4. A refining chain it helps finish is named on the card.

3. Shop

The shop always follows the event. Each player has their own offers and their own gold. Shop and Build are two tabs of the same step, so you can buy, place, and come back. Nothing is timed: the party moves on when every player has pressed Ready.

  1. Tabs for the two halves of this step.
  2. Your offers. A card shows the kind, what it needs, its rate per second (a consumer's need at top speed, an extractor's collect rate), what it does, and its price. A refinery card shows its two inputs and what it makes.
  3. Gold buys buildings and re-rolls, nothing else.
  4. Your stash: buildings you own that are not on the board. Drag here to sell.
  5. Teammates' gold and stash, for information only. You do not see their offers. Hidden in solo.
  6. What comes next. PvE options are shown ahead of time. A ghost opponent never is. When the opponent is a boss, this panel names the boss, gives one line on its mechanic and one line of counter advice, so you can build against it (the sketch shows a boss; names are placeholders).
  7. A small live view of the board that jumps to the Build tab.
  8. Ready is available on both tabs.
  9. Held run modifiers: a small chip in the run bar with the effects totalled and drawbacks marked with "!". It is on every run screen.

4. Build

Where the factory is laid out. Everything except the Core can be moved, and conduits are free. In co-op every player edits at the same time, each in their own plot; neighbouring plots are shown but cannot be edited.

  1. Stash. Drag a building onto the board to place it; placing is free.
  2. Tools. Conduit is always available and unlimited. Rotate turns the selected building. Undo covers layout changes only, never buying, selling, or re-rolling.
  3. The board. Lakes and cliffs cannot be built on. Extractors only go on resource nodes.
  4. Conduits, one resource each, coloured by resource. They cannot cross; the splitter is what lets one line feed two buildings. A conduit drawn to a border spot on the plot edge joins a neighbour's conduit at the same spot; the neighbour's half is drawn dimmed. Buildings carry their owner's tag (P1, P2), and an under-supplied consumer is marked LOW.
  5. The Core. Placed once at the start of the run; it has no move handle.
  6. The front edge, along the top. Enemy units enter along it.
  7. Preview of the routes enemy units would take to the Core with this layout.
  8. Inspector for the selected building: what it needs per second, what it is getting, and whether it is under-supplied.
  9. Overlays and problems: a dry run of resource flow, the enemy-path preview, and problems. Your own buildings are listed one by one, and each partner gets one summary line.
  10. Party panel: who is editing where, pings, and the Ready button. The party moves on when everyone is ready.
  11. In co-op each extra player adds a plot to the side. A four-player board still fits the width of a 1080p screen, and everything below the board stays free for panels.
  12. Border links: each of your own halves with its state, what it carries, and a Remove my half button.
  13. A border spot, where a conduit crosses into the neighbour's plot, with the neighbour's dimmed half beside it.
  14. A Composite chain: an Ore conduit and a Power conduit come into the refinery (Rc) from two sides, and a Composite conduit, in its own colour, goes to the Golem forge (Gf). The Foundry (Fo) also takes Ore and Power. The small text on each shows its need per second for each input (O = Ore, P = Power, F = Flux).
  15. An Aether chain: Power and Flux go into the refinery (Ra), and an Aether conduit goes to the Spire (Sr).
  16. The one Flux node feeds this chain; an Overclocker would need to share it, for example over a border link.

4b. Build: new footprints

Four new buildings (working names) with unusual footprints, so where they go is a real decision. The image shows four placement previews on the Build screen. Green cells are legal and red are not.

  1. Lancer hall (1×3, from round 2). The green column along the plot edge is a legal spot; inside the plot it needs a free strip 1 wide and 3 long.
  2. Grand barracks (3×3, from round 5). It needs a 3×3 hole, and a Merger joins two Ore extractors to feed it.
  3. Tunnel (two 1×1 ends, from round 3). The entrance is placed; the exit must be in a straight line, at most 5 cells away. Buildings and conduits in between stay where they are. A farther cell is red.
  4. Mortar (2×2, from round 3). The dashed ring is its longest range; the shaded middle is the blind spot where it cannot shoot.

5. Main menu

Where a run starts or resumes. Saved runs are listed without limit. The host of a run holds its save; guests see the run as a pointer to the host.

  1. New run starts a lobby (or goes straight in, solo). Join enters a run a friend is hosting.
  2. Saved runs. There is no limit, and each run appears once.
  3. A run you host: you can resume it, or abandon it for the whole party.
  4. A save from an older version says so before you resume: some things may have changed.
  5. A run someone else hosts. They hold the save, so you can only join when they open it. Hide removes it from your list without ending it.
  6. Records: the deepest round reached, kept per party size. Nothing else carries over between runs.

6. Core placement

The first decision of a run. The whole map is shown, and every player chooses where their own Core goes, in their own plot; other plots are dimmed. A Core cannot be moved once everyone is ready.

  1. Your own plot. Green cells are where the 2 × 2 Core fits (the cell is its lowest-left corner); red cells do not fit. A spot must lie wholly inside your plot, off water, cliffs and resource nodes, and not too close to the front edge.
  2. The front edge, where enemy units will enter.
  3. Your chosen spot, marked "Core goes here", with the routes enemy units would take to reach it: the shortest as a solid line, the others faint. Pointing at a green cell previews its routes. Click a green cell to choose it; clicking the chosen cell again changes nothing. You can pick another until you are Ready.
  4. Resource nodes. Where the rare one sits is a big part of the choice.
  5. Another player's plot, dimmed and without verdicts. Their chosen spot is outlined and carries their name.
  6. The acting-player switcher in the run bar, for playing every seat from one device (debug; only with two or more players). Each button shows "(to act)" or "(done)"; the bar also says who is still awaited. The screen always shows the acting player's view.
  7. The reason line. Pointing at a red cell says why it does not fit. A refused click or Ready (for example "Choose a spot for the Core first.", naming the player who still has to pick) shows here too, until the pointer moves to another cell or the acting player changes.
  8. Ready needs a chosen spot. Each player readies for themselves. A Ready player is offered no spots, and the button reads "Unready (change the Core)"; Unready reopens the choice. The step ends when every player has placed a Core and is Ready; solo it ends when you are Ready. There is no vote.
  9. The starting kit, waiting in the stash for the first build.

7. PvE difficulty choice

Before each PvE battle the party picks easy, medium, or hard. Each option shows what kind of enemy it is and what it pays.

  1. The stakes: losing a PvE battle costs no life, only the reward.
  2. Difficulty and enemy type. The type tells you what the battle will test.
  3. How strong the threat is.
  4. What winning pays. Harder options pay more.
  5. Votes. No time limit; you can change yours until the last player has voted, and then the battle starts.

8. Battle summary

Shown when playback ends. It says what happened and why, so the party can decide what to change.

  1. The result, and in the corner what it cost or paid: lives lost and gold gained. Battle rewards are always gold. Below it, why it ended: the step of the tiebreak ladder that decided it (here, more Cores standing), the Core health left, how many Cores stand on each side and when each Core fell.
  2. Army strength over the battle for both sides, with the moments that mattered marked, including each Core that fell.
  3. Where the enemy got through. Fallen Cores are greyed with a cross.
  4. What each building did.
  5. One line per point of interest: who held it at the end, for how long each side held it, and its buff. It is not part of the tiebreak.
  6. What comes next in the round.
  7. Watch the battle again, or ready up. The party moves on to the event when everyone is ready.

9. Run over

Every run ends in defeat; this screen says how far it got. An abandoned run ends here too.

  1. Depth reached, the score of the run.
  2. Whether it beat the party's previous best for this party size.
  3. Battle records for the run.
  4. The factory as it stood at the end.
  5. What was fought each round and how it went.
  6. Start again or go back to the menu. The run leaves the saved list; its depth and history go into your records.
